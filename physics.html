The physics of a falling cat

A cat dropped upside down lands on its feet, with nothing to push against. Here is how that's possible, from moment of inertia and angular momentum up to the exact moves the cats in the game use. The numbers on this page are computed live by the same code that runs the game.

Notation: scalars in italics (m, θ); vectors with an arrow above (L, ω, r, and the zero vector 0); unit vectors with a hat (x̂, n̂); second-rank tensors (3×3 matrices) in bold upright sans-serif (I, J). The magnitude of a vector is written without the arrow (v = |v|).

The puzzle

In 1894 Étienne-Jules Marey photographed a falling cat in a rapid series of frames, and the French Academy of Sciences argued about it. A falling cat starts with no spin, has nothing to push on, and still ends up turned over. It looked as if it broke one of the laws of mechanics. It doesn't. The cat changes its shape, and a body that can change shape can change which way it faces without ever spinning.

Gravity doesn't help or hurt the turn. It pulls on every part of the cat in proportion to that part's mass, so its torque about the centre of mass is

τg = Σ ri × mig = (Σ miri) × g = 0 because Σ miri = 0 when ri is measured from the centre of mass

Gravity only sets how long the cat has before it hits the ground.

Rotation in classical mechanics

Newton's second law for straight-line motion, F = dp/dt, has a twin for rotation:

τ = dL/dt torque = rate of change of angular momentum, both taken about the centre of mass

Both are vectors: they have a direction (the axis of the turning) as well as a size. Torque τ plays the role of force, and angular momentum L plays the role of momentum. With no torque, L cannot change, in size or direction. A cat dropped without spin has L = 0, and in free fall (gravity gives no torque about the centre of mass, and air drag on a falling cat gives very little) it keeps L = 0 all the way down. Taking moments about the centre of mass is what makes this work even though the cat is accelerating downward.

The catch: L = 0 does not mean the cat can't change its orientation. For a rigid body it would: L = Iω, and the inertia tensor I is positive definite (invertible), so L = 0 forces ω = 0. A cat isn't rigid.

Moment of inertia

Moment of inertia is to rotation what mass is to straight-line motion: how hard a body is to set spinning. Unlike mass, it is not a single number. In three dimensions it is a tensor, a 3×3 symmetric matrix, because how hard a body is to spin depends on the axis. For point masses at positions ri from the reference point:

I = Σ mi ( |ri|2 1 − ri riT ),   i.e.   Ijk = Σ mi ( ri2 δjk − ri,j ri,k ) 1 = identity, δjk = Kronecker delta

The scalar "moment of inertia about an axis" is one number taken from this tensor. For the axis along the unit vector n̂:

In = n̂ · I n̂ = Σ mi r⊥i2 r⊥i = perpendicular distance of each mass from that axis

Because the distance is squared, mass far out counts a lot. A figure skater spins faster by pulling in their arms. A cat makes half of its body "light" or "heavy" to rotate by tucking or spreading that half's legs. In a frame lined up with a body's symmetry axes the tensor is diagonal; its three diagonal entries (the principal moments) are scalars.

The model cats are built from solid cylinders (the body halves) and point masses (the legs). A solid cylinder of mass m, radius r and length ℓ, about its centre in its own frame (x along its axis):

Icyl = diag( ½ m r2,  m(3r2 + ℓ2)/12,  m(3r2 + ℓ2)/12 ) principal moments: about its long axis, and across it

When the half turns relative to the cat, its tensor turns with it: I′ = R I RT, where R is the half's rotation matrix. That is what makes it a tensor rather than just a table of numbers. A leg of mass m at perpendicular distance d from the half's long axis adds m d2 to the moment about that axis (and, because the legs hang below the spine rather than around it, off-diagonal "products of inertia" to the full tensor).

Angular momentum and its conservation

For a single rigid body with angular velocity ω, angular momentum about its centre of mass is the inertia tensor acting on the angular velocity vector:

L = I ω a matrix times a vector, so L need not point along ω unless ω lies along a principal axis

A cat is several bodies (body halves, legs) moving relative to one another. Its total angular momentum about its centre of mass adds up every part's own spin plus each part's motion around the centre:

L = Σi [ Ii ωi + mi ri × vi ] = 0 Ii: part i's inertia tensor about its own centre of mass; ωi: its angular velocity; ri, vi: position and velocity of its centre of mass relative to the cat's

This is a vector equation, so it is three equations at once: zero angular momentum about every axis.

This is the one law the whole game is built on. When the cat moves part of its body one way, the rest of it has to turn the other way, just enough to keep the sum at zero. Which way, and how much, depends on the moments of inertia of the parts.

On its own, a change of shape that's later undone gives back exactly what it took: bend and unbend, and you end up facing the same way. A net turn needs a loop of shapes in which the way out and the way back differ.

Move 1: tuck and turn

The simplest way to turn over (model A's only move, described by Rademaker and ter Braak in the 1930s):

  1. Tuck the front legs and spread the back legs. The front half is now light (small I) and the back half heavy.
  2. Twist the spine. With the spine straight, both halves turn about the same axis, the spine (unit vector x̂), so only the component of L along it matters, and that component must stay zero: IFωF + IBωB = 0. Here IF = x̂·IFx̂ is a scalar (the moment about the spine) and ωF = ωF·x̂ a scalar rate (the component along it). So the light front half turns a long way and the heavy back half only a little.
  3. Swap: spread the front legs, tuck the back legs.
  4. Untwist. Now the back half is light, so this time it does most of the turning. The front half barely turns back.

Each half turned further one way than it came back, so the whole cat is left rotated. Integrate the constraint over a twist θ (the relative angle φF − φB) between a light half of moment IL and a heavy half IH: the light half turns θ IH/(IL + IH) and the heavy half −θ IL/(IL + IH). Untwisting with the roles swapped and adding up gives the net roll per cycle:

Δφ = θ (IH − IL) / (IH + IL) exact if each half's moment about the spine is fixed and the spine stays straight (the legs move only at the swaps)

Move 2: bend and twist

Kane and Scher (1969) modelled the cat as two cylinders joined at the spine. Bend the spine into a V, then sweep the direction of the bend around in a circle, like a hula hoop: belly side, one flank, back, the other flank, belly again.

Kane and Scher solved this exactly for two identical, axially symmetric halves bent by 2β. One full lap of the sweep rolls the whole cat by:

Δψ = 2π ( A cos β / (A cos2β + B sin2β) − 1 ) A, B: a half's principal moments (scalars) about its own axis and across it, through its own centre of mass

For a solid cylinder B/A = ½ + ℓ2/(6r2) ≥ ½, which makes the fraction less than 1, so Δψ is negative: the body rolls the opposite way to the sweep. For a small bend it is about −2πβ2(B/A − ½), which is why the roll grows with the square of the bend.

A cat is long and thin, so A is smaller than B (about a third for model B's body), and a deep bend gives a big roll. Tucking the legs shrinks A further, which is why the computer optimizer, and now the game's reflex, tucks all four legs while sweeping. The game's engine reproduces this formula to within 0.0003° (see the results page).

Why a loop of shapes gives a turn

Picture every shape the cat can make as a point in a "shape space": spine bend, bend direction, twist, leg positions. A righting move is a path through that space that starts and ends at the same shape. Shapere and Wilczek (1989) showed that the net turn depends only on the path, not on how fast the cat moves along it. It's a geometric effect, a cousin of the Berry phase in quantum mechanics and the reason a parallel-parked car can move sideways.

Mathematically, L = 0 gives the body's rotation rate as a function of the shape and its rate of change: Ω = −J(s)−1h(s, ṡ), linear in ṡ. The turn produced by a small change of shape depends on which shape you are in when you make it. In the language of differential geometry this "connection" has curvature: the turn is not the change of any function of the shape, so going around a loop does not bring it back to zero. Moving back along the same path cancels the turn; moving around a loop leaves something over, roughly the curvature times the area the loop encloses.

This does not need rotations to fail to commute. Putterman and Raz's "square cat" rotates itself in a flat plane, where all rotations commute, by exactly this effect. In three dimensions the non-commuting rotations add extra terms on top, which is one reason the exact Kane–Scher result above is not just a simple area formula.

Move 3: the leg pump (pitch)

Bend and twist fixes the roll (rotation about the spine). A cat can also be tipped nose up or nose down (pitch). The game's cats fix it with tuck and turn applied to the bend instead of the twist:

  1. Tuck one pair of legs: that half becomes light.
  2. Bend toward the belly. The light half swings further than the heavy half.
  3. Swap which legs are tucked.
  4. Straighten. Now the other half swings further, so the halves don't return to where they started and the body is left tipped.

Tucking the front legs first tips the nose down; tucking the back legs first tips it up. One full pump turns the cat by … and takes about 0.4 s, so the cats do it only when there's time left after rolling upright.

A whole righting, step by step

This is what the cats in Watch mode do, and what the narrator panel describes as it happens:

  1. Sensing the fall (about 50–100 ms). The inner ear notices free fall. Until the shape changes, nothing turns.
  2. Bend and twist, legs tucked. A deep bend swept around against the roll still needed. This does most of the turning.
  3. Easing in. With less than about 45° to go, the bend eases off (roll per lap grows roughly with the bend squared, so a full bend would overshoot) and the legs come half out.
  4. Leg pump, if the nose is tipped and there's time.
  5. Ready to land. Straight spine, legs spread, knees nearly straight (models C and D) for the longest cushioning stroke.
  6. Touchdown. The legs absorb the impact (below).

Model A can't bend, so it repeats tuck-and-turn cycles instead. Each cycle also tips its nose a little, because the legs hang below the spine, so it alternates front-first and back-first cycles so the tips cancel. In model D the flexible front spine does most of the work: the optimizer found that turning the front half first, with its legs tucked, lands from lower drops.

How much time there is

Ignoring air drag, a drop from height h takes

t = √(2h/g) g = 9.81 m/s²

so 1 m gives 0.45 s and 30 cm gives 0.25 s. The game's cats need about 0.37 s for a perfect righting from upside down, close to what real cats manage (0.3–0.4 s), plus their reaction delay.

Landing, impact and air drag

On touchdown the paws stop, but the body keeps moving and sinks while the legs fold, over a distance d (the stroke). Stopping from speed v (= |v|) over d at a steady rate takes a deceleration of magnitude a = v²/2d, pointing up. The ground must supply that plus the cat's weight, N = m(g + a), so the load in body weights (N/mg) is:

impact = 1 + v2/(2g d) = 1 + h/d body weights all magnitudes; second form: no air drag, so v2 = 2gh

That second form is striking: the impact is just 1 plus drop height divided by stopping distance. Rigid legs stop the body in about 2 cm, so a 1 m drop means about 50× body weight. Model C's jointed legs fold over about 16 cm, which is about 7×. That's why a cat lands with straight legs and lets them fold.

Air drag and terminal speed

Air pushes back against the motion. As a vector the drag force points opposite the velocity:

Fd = −½ ρ CdA |v| v,    so   Fd = |Fd| = ½ ρ CdA v2

Its size grows with the square of the speed until it balances the cat's weight mg. At that terminal speed the cat stops speeding up:

vt = √(2mg / (ρ CdA)) ρ = 1.2 kg/m³ for air

For a 4 kg cat with spread legs (CdA ≈ 0.09 m²) that's about 27 m/s (60 mph), and tucked (0.05 m²) about 36 m/s. Spreading the legs works like a parachute. The approach is gradual: a cat falling 7 floors (21 m) is already at about two-thirds of terminal speed, and from higher floors the speed barely climbs. The results page (high-rise falls) shows that speed alone doesn't explain why vets saw fewer injuries in cats falling from higher.

Zero gravity

In a parabolic-flight cabin everything floats, cat included. The cat can still turn by changing shape, exactly as in a fall. The problem is knowing which way to turn. The inner ear has two kinds of sensor. The semicircular canals sense rotation, and they still work. The otoliths sense linear acceleration relative to free fall (gravity minus the body's own acceleration), which is zero when everything falls together. So the cat can still feel itself turning but can't feel which way is down, and "down" has to come from somewhere else: seeing the floor, or remembering where down was. In the game, the cat steers toward where it believes down is (the white ring on the gauge). If it can see the floor, it stays feet-down; if its sense of down is gone, it turns and turns without getting anywhere.

How the simulation computes it

The engine never applies a turning force to the cat. At every time step (every 0.5 ms of simulated time) it looks at how each body part moved because of the change of shape, and works out the one rotation of the whole cat that keeps the total angular momentum at zero:

J(s) Ω = −h(s, ṡ)

Here s is the list of shape settings (bend, bend direction, twist, legs, knees). It is a point in shape space, not a vector in physical space. The other symbols:

  • J = Σ [ Ii + mi(|ri|21 − ririT) ] is the whole cat's inertia tensor about its centre of mass in its current shape, with each part's tensor rotated into the cat frame (R I RT).
  • h = Σ [ Iiwi + mi ri × dri/dt ] is the angular momentum the shape change alone would carry, where wi and dri/dt are each part's motion relative to the cat frame.
  • Ω is the cat frame's angular velocity, the unknown.

Solving that 3×3 linear system gives Ω, and the orientation matrix is advanced by R ← R exp([Ω]×Δt), where [Ω]× is the skew-symmetric matrix with [Ω]×x = Ω × x (the matrix exponential is a rotation by |Ω|Δt about Ω). The |L| readout in the game then measures angular momentum independently from the motion of every part, as a check; it stays at rounding-error level.
