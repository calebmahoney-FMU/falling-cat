Falling Cat: what the simulations found

Four studies run on the game's physics engine (back to the game). Every number here was computed by the same code that runs the cat in the game.

Picks the heat map's cat, and which cat stands out in the charts that compare all four.

1 · Is the engine right?

For the classic Kane–Scher cat (two identical cylinders, no legs), sweeping the spine's bend direction once around has an exact answer: Δroll = 360° · (A cos β / (A cos²β + B sin²β) − 1), with β half the bend angle and A, B each half's principal moments of inertia (scalars, the diagonal entries of its inertia tensor) about its own axis and across it, through its own centre of mass. The engine never uses this formula: it integrates the full vector equation L = 0 with each body's 3×3 inertia tensor, so matching it is an independent check.

2 · Survival map

Thousands of drops with the cat's built-in reflex (roll upright, then pump out any nose-up/down tilt, then spread its legs), covering every starting roll, pitch, height and reaction delay, with air drag. The cat is left-right symmetric, so rolls from 0° to 180° cover every case.

3 · Can a computer find a better move?

An evolution strategy (CMA-ES) searched timed sequences of all the body controls (spine bend, bend direction, twist, both pairs of legs and, for models C and D, knees) for the lowest drop the cat can still land perfectly from: feet within 20° of straight down, spine straight, legs spread. No reaction delay, with air drag.

4 · High-rise falls

Upside-down drops from half a metre to 30 floors (3 m per floor), 60 ms reflex. With air, a falling cat's speed levels off toward a terminal speed: spread legs act like a parachute and cap it near 27 m/s (60 mph).

5 · Zero gravity

In the 1940s and 50s, cats were flown on parabolic flights, giving about 20 s of weightlessness each time. Films of those flights show the cats struggling to orient themselves: with no gravity, nothing tells them which way is down. Here each cat floats in a cabin for 20 s from a random tilt, and its reflex steers toward where it believes down is: the cabin floor it can see, a memory of down that slowly fades (drifts at random), or no sense of down at all, so it keeps settling on a new random "down".
